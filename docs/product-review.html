Ember

Product review · accountability engine · reviewed against the source, 20 Jun 2026

Verdict. Ember has one genuinely differentiated idea (capture is solved, follow-through is the product) and executes the easy two-thirds of it well: the date-first ranking in compareActionable is clean IP, and the burn-to-ash completion is the rare to-do app moment that actually feels good. But the one feature that would make someone pay, the referee escalation, is the thinnest part of the build: a self-served wa.me deep link with no real stake and a referee who is never actually in the loop. As a product it currently competes with free to-do apps on the things it does worse (capture, reminders, breadth) while under-shipping the thing only it promises (consequences).

Biggest risk: Ember is an n=1 tool wearing a product costume. Every layer assumes one hardcoded person, the single ownerChatId in ensureOwner, the referee enum baked to wife | sister | colleague, and a classifier system prompt that literally opens "You are Ember, a personal accountability agent for Jules." Worse than the rebuild cost: the accountability mechanic has no counterparty engaged, so the moat is a prototype. Decide first whether you are hardening a personal tool or building a product. They pull in opposite directions.

Improve Features that work but underdeliver Ranked P0 → P2.

1.

Close the referee loop

P0Effort L

Escalation is Ember's whole reason to exist, and right now it is a button that drafts a WhatsApp message to yourself to send. In nudge.ts, buttons() puts a waLink() referee tap first on the escalate tier, and escalationDraft() writes a sharp message. But the referee never receives anything unless you choose to forward it, and they have no view, no confirmation, no way to nudge back. The counterparty in an accountability product is passive and uninformed.

ProblemThe social cost is opt-in and self-administered, so the easiest move is to never tap. Beeminder and StickK work because the stake fires whether or not you feel like it, and a real person is watching. ChangeMake the referee an actual node. Minimum: a one-tap that genuinely sends (bot-to-bot, or a generated referee link showing the one overdue item and a "poke him" button). Better: the referee confirms completion, so "done" is not self-graded. ImpactThis is the only thing a free to-do app cannot copy. It is the difference between a nicer Todoist and a product with a moat.
2.

Make the streak legible and actually reward it

P1Effort S

currentStreak in streak.ts is well-reasoned (a day breaks it only if something fell due and went uncleared), but the rule is invisible to the user. The chip in page.tsx shows a number and a tooltip. The single most reliable daily-return mechanic in the category is sitting there under-dramatized.

ProblemA streak you do not understand and barely see does not pull you back tomorrow. Habitica and Streaks make the number loud and the "you will lose it tonight" moment explicit. ChangeSurface what is protecting the streak and what threatens it ("clear the gym by tonight to keep 6"). Mark the moment it grows. Put the at-risk signal in the evening nudge, which already runs. ImpactHighest retention-per-hour on the board. The data is already computed; this is presentation, not new infrastructure.
3.

Deadline-aware nudge timing

P1Effort M

Two fixed crons in vercel.json fire at 09:00 and 21:00 HKT. runSweep sends the single top-ranked item. So a task you set for "today at 3pm" is invisible until the next window, and there is no reminder at the actual moment a thing is due.

ProblemA reminder that ignores the time of the deadline is a worse reminder than the default phone clock. Right now Ember is strictly weaker than the OS at "ping me when it is due." ChangeWithin the constraint: let the morning nudge name same-day items with a target time, and lean the evening check on those. To truly ping at the deadline you need more than two crons (see the callout). ImpactCloses the most obvious gap a new user feels in week one against any reminders app.
4.

First-run activation

P1Effort M

/start in the Telegram route returns one dense paragraph of commands. The mental model (type is derived, the "death zone," referees) is never taught; it is encoded in the classifier prompt and left for the user to infer.

ProblemFor a messaging product the first session is where most users leave. A wall of syntax on message one is the opposite of activation. ChangeGuide the first commitment in three taps: "text me one thing you keep avoiding" → it classifies and shows the result → "pick who holds you to it." One captured, referee-attached item is the activation event. ImpactTurns the strongest part of the build (the classifier) into the onboarding instead of hiding it behind a command list.

Add New features that broaden value Each tied to a job-to-be-done and a retention or activation mechanism.

1.

Weekly receipts

P1Effort M

The append-only Event table already logs done, nudged, snoozed, promised. Nothing reads it back to the user. That is a reflection product waiting in the data you are already writing.

JTBD"Show me I am actually changing, not just being nagged." ChangeA Sunday message and a board panel: cleared vs. dodged, longest streak, the item you pushed the most, kept-promise rate. Built from the Event table, no new infra. RetentionThe weekly review is the habit that makes people keep a paid tool. A nag earns resentment; receipts earn loyalty. This is the single best argument for a subscription.
2.

Real stakes (opt-in teeth)

P1Effort M

Ember already gestures at consequences (the blunt copy, the referee-first button) without ever imposing one. The category's paying users (Beeminder, StickK) pay precisely because the stake is real.

JTBD"Make the cost of skipping bigger than the cost of doing it." ChangeOptional, per item: a referee who must confirm before "done" counts, or a streak-freeze economy where breaking it actually costs something visible. Start with social stakes (no payments) so it stays inside your constraints. RetentionLoss aversion is the strongest lever in the category and the one Ember is closest to already having. Pairs naturally with the referee-loop improvement above.
3.

Commit templates / quick presets

P2Effort S

Activation depends on getting a few real commitments in fast. Today every item is a free-text round trip to Haiku.

JTBD"Get me set up without thinking up my whole life right now." ChangeA handful of one-tap starters with sensible cadence + referee baked in (weekly workout, monthly finances review, the call you keep dodging). Pure board UI over createItem. ActivationEmpty-state-to-three-items is where to-do apps live or die. This is the cheapest fix for it.
4.

Paired accountability

P2Effort L

The honest answer to "could others pay" is that the highest-value version is two people holding each other to it, which is also why Focusmate sells. This is the strategic bet, flagged in the callout because it breaks the single-user constraint.

JTBD"Pair me with someone who will actually notice if I flake." ChangeTwo linked users, each the other's referee, each seeing the other's one burning item. Requires real multi-tenant auth (today it is one ownerChatId). GrowthBuilt-in virality: every active user invites at least one partner. No other feature here brings its own distribution.

Remove Noise: low value, confusing, or upkeep > payoff Kill-biased. Ranked by how much cutting it sharpens the product.

1.

The parking-lot staleness ceremony

P1Effort S

parkingAgeLabel, isStaleParking, STALE_PARKING_DAYS, and the "Decide: date it or drop it" flag exist to manage a drawer that is, by design, where undated things rot. You built machinery to feel bad about a feature that contradicts the thesis.

Why killA follow-through engine that ships a junk drawer dilutes its own promise. The "death zone" logic in the classifier already knows undated-important is the failure mode, so do not give it a comfortable home on the board. CutEither drop parking entirely and force a date at capture, or keep a bare inbox and delete the age/stale ceremony. Less code, sharper product. CautionYou are attached to this (the recent commits add board create and stale flags). That is the reason to look at it hard, not a reason to keep it.
2.

The daily empty-state morning ping

P1Effort S

In runSweep, the morning slot sends "Nothing pressing. Clean slate." even when there is nothing to do. The evening check correctly stays silent; the morning does not.

Why killA notification that fires on quiet days is how you teach a user to swipe your bot away unread. The value of the nudge is that it only speaks when it matters. The empty ping spends that trust daily. CutStay silent when nothing is due, same as evening. Or fold "clean slate" into the weekly receipts where it reads as a win, not as noise.
3.

The three-tier defer warning

P2Effort S

deferState grades pushes into low | high | alarm with orange, red, and a pulsing red icon, plus a nudge line past two. The signal ("you keep dodging this") is worth keeping. The three-tier visual production around it is not.

Why trimAn escalating animated guilt meter is a lot of surface for one bit of information. For a product, nagging the user about how much they are being nagged tips from motivating into punishing. CutOne state: "pushed N times." Keep the count, drop the tiers and the pulse. Less CSS, same message.
4.

The sticky six-category filter

P2Effort S

The category chips in page.tsx hold a sticky bar at the top of the board. The taxonomy is fixed and personal (CATEGORIES maps business → "Build," etc.) and the whole product premise is to act on the one item at the top, not to browse by life area.

Why killPrime real estate spent on a slicing tool that fights the "do the next thing" thesis. Filtering by category is a planning gesture; Ember is a doing tool. The categories are also the most obviously single-user thing on screen. CutKeep the colored dot for at-a-glance context, drop the sticky filter bar. If you keep filtering, demote it off the default view.

Worth breaking a constraint for

More than two scheduled wake-ups

Vercel Hobby caps you at two crons a day, which is why nudges are stuck at 09:00 and 21:00 and why a deadline-time reminder is impossible. Two real fixes: a single cron that runs every few minutes against a due-time queue (one cron, many checks), or a small external scheduler (Upstash QStash, a GitHub Action) that hits your endpoint per-item.

TradeoffA bit of infra and a queue concept on top of the flat table, or a few dollars a month off Hobby. In exchange you get "ping me when it is actually due," which is table stakes for any paid reminder product and the one gap a new user feels immediately.

Multi-tenant auth (the product-vs-tool fork)

Paired accountability and any version of "others pay" require lifting the single-ownerChatId model. This is the expensive one, and it is genuinely a different product. Do not do it to be safe; do it only if you decide Ember is a business, not a personal tool. Stated plainly so the cost is on the table before any feature pretends to bridge it.

TradeoffA real rebuild (auth, per-user scoping everywhere, the referee enum becomes a relationship). The upside is the only path to distribution and revenue. The downside is months of work that a personal tool does not need.

The three things I'd do first

1

Close the referee loop

It is the only feature a free to-do app cannot copy, and right now it does not actually involve the referee. Until escalation produces a real consequence with a real person, Ember is a nicer Todoist competing on Todoist's terms. Fix the moat before polishing anything around it.

2

Ship weekly receipts

The Event table is already recording everything; nothing reads it back. Reflection is what converts a nag people tolerate into a tool people renew. Lowest new-infra cost, highest claim on a subscription, and it makes the streak and defer signals finally mean something cumulative.

3

Cut the noise before adding anything

Kill the empty morning ping, the parking staleness ceremony, and the three-tier defer meter. Each one spends user trust or contradicts the thesis. A follow-through engine earns its authority by speaking only when it matters. Protect that signal first; it is cheap and it makes everything above land harder.
